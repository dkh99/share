A two-minute customer story. Deadpan testimonial, filmed in Sedgeham village hall and the field behind it.

The principle. A man who has never written a line of code asks Code Overlord where to put the stalls at the village fete, and its agents build him an app. He calls it Bunthorne, and he keeps going back to have it wired into one more real thing: the tannoy, a board in the hall window, the milk order. The fete takes eight times what it took the year it rained, and by the end Bunthorne runs the day with his laptop shut at home.

Why it is funny. Everything he asks for arrives exactly as asked, and the fete gets better on the strength of it. Then he goes back one last time, with the one question nobody in an English village has ever been able to answer, and Bunthorne answers it.

A two-minute customer story. Deadpan testimonial, filmed on a trading estate outside Norwich.

Accent. The whole cast is broad Norfolk, Precious included, but she talks like a policy document. Nobody in it is joking.

The principle. Precious runs the day shift at a warehouse. Her boss asked her once, in passing, for a team-building exercise. She had never written a line of code, so she asked Code Overlord, and its agents built her an app she called Everyone Joins In. It worked, so she kept asking it for more exercises, and more often, until a different one ran every ten minutes, everybody in the building loved each other, and nobody did any work.

Why it is funny. The engine is success. Every exercise works, so she adds another and runs it more often, and the app never runs the same one twice. What it costs is the one thing the building is for, and nobody minds.

"Which people?"

"Everyone. It's in the name."

"It sounds like you're carrying a lot, Gary."

"I'll fall on my sword. I resign."

"Precious."

The klaxon goes. The screen flicks over: TELL THE PERSON ON YOUR LEFT YOU LOVE THEM. John closes his mouth and turns to his left, which is the Tesco driver.

"I love you too, John."

"He'll want the log-in."

"This is Everyone Joins In. Please hug the nearest person."

"I typed one sentence into Code Overlord. Everyone Joins In has never done the same one twice." A pause. "You can't fault the software."

5. The engine

Sibling to Bruce Almighty, from the premises catalogue: given limitless power, a person uses it on tiny petty things first, and here only on tiny petty things. The grudge underneath it. Dawn does not really want the lane. She wants Graham to say he was in it. He never will. 7. Structure, and why

The spine is the Circle, Dan Harmon's eight steps: You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change. The engine inside it is the Game of the Scene: the one unusual thing, her small ask changing the next thousand years, heightened and explored in every sequence. That is the combination story-structures.md calls the strongest available: the game supplies the laughs beat by beat, the circle supplies the shape and the ending.

Why this and not the others:

  • Not the Snowball on its own. Every short we have made is a Snowball, and a Snowball saturates. Heighten one thing for a hundred minutes and it hits the point where more stops being funnier. Dawn's builds are still a snowball, but they sit inside a circle, so there is somewhere to come back to.
  • The circle was built for this. Harmon made it for short comedy and then ran two high-concept comedies on it, one of them about a man dragged across time and space by a scientist. It is proven on exactly this kind of story.
  • Eight steps, eight sequences. One step per sequence, twelve to fifteen minutes each, and the whole thing is legible on one listen.
  • Two people travel it in opposite directions. He needs something to happen. She needs nothing to happen in her lane. By the end each has got the other's wish.
  • The last shot rhymes with the first, same desk, same telephone, and it means the opposite. That rhyme is the ending's last laugh.

The last two sequences run as a farce convergence, and everybody who arrives on that school field is brought there by their own grudge or their own ask. The one exception is the Prime Minister, and he is there because somebody put him on the list.

The tone bar is Taking Back Tuesday. A steady build of smaller laughs, every minute climbing towards a few huge ones. Each sequence below names the huge laugh it is building to. The biggest in the film, in order of size:

  1. The helicopter, sequence eight. "Can we go?" "She hasn't said." It is Benidorm paying off with a Prime Minister in it, and it climbs from there until the head of the government is made to say "Thank you, Mrs Mercer."
  2. "Can he go?" "I haven't said.", sequence four. A man at a hotel pool door at noon, burnt down one side, because she will not look at him.
  3. The scan. "He wants his head looking at", sequence two, paid off fifty minutes later on Question Time: "I'd like to thank her for the scan." A setup at minute twenty that pays off at minute seventy is the thing a feature can do and a short cannot.

Behind those three: the pigs, and the boarding pass.

9. The ending

The film leaves the ground in both centuries at once, and the software never fails in either.

In 2026 the leaving is literal: a pensioner breaststroking over the Black Country in a rig first tested on pigs, and the Prime Minister held in the sky by a lollipop and a four-word Act of Parliament, then landed in a long-jump pit and walked across the road with Year 3. Two ending families run together. Absurd success, played completely flat: a lollipop lady has halted the head of the government in mid-air, and nobody on the field treats that as the remarkable part; the remarkable part, to everybody there, is that she has her crossing back. And she overrules the machine. Its answer was correct, and she says so, and she names it: Code Overlord has never been wrong once. The slow object was in the wrong lane of the sky. She rejected it for a human reason, in two words she first said in minute ten about a boy with a worm: "He's allowed."

In 3026 the leaving is the circle closing. Aldous climbs out of his box at the Department of Incidents and walks back to his desk past the Hall of Finished Things, where the last words on the old screen no longer say That'll do. They say Go on, then. Out of the window, people are flying: in lanes, in coloured wristbands, some of them pigs, in the first rain for six hundred years, and everybody is complaining about it. 10. How it sells Code Overlord

Nobody explains anything and nobody names a feature. By the end, a viewer who has never heard of the product knows this:

  • You say what you want in your own words, and it builds you a working thing that keeps working without you. A woman who has never made anything writes one sentence on a list and by teatime there is a system with a name, wristbands coming off a printer, a lane on the wall.
  • You can keep asking, and it keeps building, as big as you like. One lane, one building, one country, the sky. Every rung is her asking again, and every rung is another thing that exists.
  • It reaches out of the laptop into the real world. Printers, couriers, laminated signs, a card payment, a boarding pass, a private scan, an independent review with a hundred and forty submissions, a rig with eight propellers. Whatever she built, strangers used without knowing who made it.
  • One ask can put a lot of workers on it at once, and it runs on a schedule while you sleep or fly. Windows opening one under another on the screen; a job that picks up the list every quarter of an hour; the phone saying it is running remotely at thirty thousand feet.
  • It lives on your phone as well as your desk. The list is on both, which is the entire plot.
  • Anybody can use it. A lollipop lady ran a country with it. An eleven-year-old built the machine in his maths book with it before lunch.
  • It does exactly what you ask. It never fails, not once, in a hundred minutes. Everything that goes wrong is what somebody asked for, and the last word said about it on screen is that it has never been wrong once. That is the most flattering thing a comedy can say about a piece of software.

The origin story is the brand's own. The name, the Summon button and the whole dark-lord vocabulary come from a bored man in 3026 who localised it from the one paperback that survived, and the way it reached the world is a lollipop lady writing let everybody have a go. The company's own story, a product from 3026 sent to improve the present, becomes a film in which it did, in the most Black Country way available.

What a Code Overlord user sees and nobody else does: the notepad down the side of the screen, the same notepad on the phone, the photograph attached to it, and the section at the bottom where finished things go; a job on a quarter-hourly cadence whose instruction is readable, do whatever is on the list; windows opening one under another beneath a single ask; the phone app showing the sessions working, one of them running remotely; the pairing code on the screen before the parcel goes.

How this was made

Written from the skill's references and the eleven short films, then read twice by a panel of five comedians, one agent each, in character, blind to each other, all asked the client's question verbatim and given Taking Back Tuesday as the tonal bar. The five: Armando Iannucci, deadpan and long-form, for what committee or form this would generate and whether the plot keeps making new scenes for a hundred minutes; Larry David, long-form, for the petty grievance pursued with legal seriousness; Tim Robinson, for the exit nobody takes; Jimmy Carr, for whether the huge laughs are huge and the funny word comes last; Victoria Wood, long-form and warmth, for Black Country speech and whether anybody loves anybody.

Round one verdict: charming, not hilarious. All five found the laughs real and the register right, and all five said the same three things: the huge laughs were pictures rather than jokes (four hundred towels landing is a gasp, not a laugh); Dawn got her empty lane at minute twenty-two and then became a passenger while Aldous wrote her asks; and the middle was a tour, with a montage of the short films that nobody in a cinema would understand. What changed: Dawn now writes every ask herself and wants Graham to admit he was in her way (Robinson), which became the engine; Graham trained her, lives next door and does not know her in a swimming cap (Wood, David); Wayne Pritchard and "I haven't said" (David); the scan, the heat map, the pigs, the Pigeon Protocol, the Select Committee and Question Time as a written scene (Wood, Robinson, Carr, Iannucci); the mother sleeves as the last rung (Robinson); the cameo montage, the Cabinet, the dictation gag, the cup of tea and "Warmly" cut.

Round two verdict: much funnier. Carr: "Much. About twice as funny." David: "Yes. A lot. Somebody finally gave her a grudge she can't win." Iannucci: "Last time the film had a mood." Now it has an engine. Robinson named the biggest laugh as "Can he go?" "I haven't said.", and Wood called it exactly "For one bin?" "At this stage." Against Taking Back Tuesday, the panel's reading is that the feature now has bigger peaks: the helicopter chain and the Benidorm door both beat "At this stage", and Carr counted the scan as this film's "Quite a lot of Dave." Where it was still safe, and what changed: the sorry chorus was a hymn in the middle of a set piece (four of five), so Graham now says sorry once, to the wrong man; the section-two line was a tag on a tag, so it is now "Whose law?" "Yours."; sequences five and six were overstuffed, so the Whitehall run went; Aldous was a man at a window, so he now sets up an independent review that arrives in twenty minutes; sequence seven opened on the stock alien-in-the-rain image, so it now opens on pork scratchings and the council's hold music; the one face-to-face meeting was summarised, so it is written; and the ending ended six times, so the reporter and the skywriter went, and the scrap of paper now makes the telephone matter.

Contradictions, and who won. Wayne's apology: David's "What Vectra?" beat Iannucci's lollipop lowered an inch and Carr's "Do I know you?", because a woman who has kept a registration for fifteen years pretending she has never heard of the car is funnier than her accepting, and it pays off "Do you know him?" "No." from Benidorm. Graham's last question: David's "Yes. Go on, then." "Fair enough." beat Robinson's and Wood's "No", because she only gets the admission by saying it herself and he stays delighted, which is funnier; the warmth moved into the credits truce. "I'm a pilot": cut, with Carr and Wood, against Iannucci and Robinson who laughed at it, because the pilot's face at the climax is worth more than a wink fifty minutes early. The pigs stayed, with four of five calling them huge, and Robinson's fix went in: the laugh now lands on Dawn assessing forty pigs as slow. The Tenby weather rung and rain on one side of the street were not taken, because stopping the weather is the spent ending of It Didn't Rain. The German family in the sunlounger queue went, because two readers called the joke tired. David's Gary Pritchard is renamed Wayne, because Gary is already a name in two of the short films.

The one note still open. Robinson's warning that a list which does everything literally makes the machine a genie, with the audience a step ahead of it. The film now puts the laugh on what Dawn does after each literal delivery rather than on the delivery, but the screenplay should keep testing every list gag against his question: who is choosing here, and is it her?

Three things to decide

  1. The title. Left With Neighbour, recommended, over Wait Till I Say, which gives away the climax.
  2. The pigs. A city farm's pigs in harnesses under propellers are the most talked-about picture in the film and the test flight for the ending. Recommended as written, gently, at head height, in no hurry, with a vet in the credits, because a pig that looks mistreated stops being funny in the same second.
  3. The Prime Minister. Recommended unnamed, of no party and never a likeness, because the joke is the office being made to say "Thank you, Mrs Mercer", not a person, and it has to survive a change of government before the film is released.
